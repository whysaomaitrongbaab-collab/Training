## 2. Why we "rent" a computer (GPU) instead of using our own

**GPU** (graphics card) is a computer part that can do huge numbers of calculations at the same time, very fast — it's normally used for gaming/video editing, but AI needs it for calculating too (because AI is multiplying and adding numbers millions upon millions of times, over and over).

The AI model we use (Qwen3.6) is huge — it has a whopping **35,000 million** "parameters" (the numbers the model uses to make decisions). The GPU in an ordinary computer (e.g. the RTX 5060 you have, with 8GB of VRAM) can't hold a model this size at all; you need a card with **at least 74GB of VRAM (the graphics card's memory)** — a card at this level costs hundreds of thousands of THB to buy outright, so we chose to **"rent" it by the hour** instead (like renting a car — no need to buy one outright) through the Vast.ai website — you only pay while you're actually using it ($1.056/h for the GPU rented today).

## 3. What is SSH, and why not use the web (Jupyter)

**SSH** is a way to "connect in and control another computer from far away" through a terminal (the black screen where you type commands) — it's like being able to sit at home but type commands that control a computer in another country.

At first we tried connecting through **Jupyter** (a web page where you can type code), but kept hitting a "won't open" problem, over and over, many times. After looking into it, we found that people who seriously train AI all over the world mainly use SSH, because it's more stable, faster, and comes with **`tmux`** (a program that lets you close the window while the job keeps running on the rented machine, so you don't have to sit watching the screen the whole time).

**SSH key:** instead of typing a password every time, we create 2 "digital keys" — one (private) is kept on your computer, the other (public) is handed to Vast.ai for safekeeping. When you connect to the rented machine, the two keys check each other to see whether they match — it's safer than a password, and you do it once and it works forever.

## 4. What is Unsloth — why you don't need to "go into" it like a website

This is a really good question, because it's a common misunderstanding: **Unsloth isn't a website or an app that you have to log into to use**.

Unsloth is a **"library"** — think of it like **a cookbook sitting on a shelf**. When we want to cook (write a program), we just "take the book down and open it" (type the command `import unsloth` in Python code) and can use the recipes in it right away — no signing up, no login, we don't even need to go online to reach the Unsloth company at all (only when installing it the first time do you need to download the files and keep them on the machine, like buying the book and putting it on the shelf once — after that you can keep taking it down and using it whenever you like).

**What Unsloth does:** it makes training an AI model (fine-tuning) **use less VRAM and run faster**, by using specialized techniques (e.g. managing memory more cleverly), compared with writing the model-training code ourselves from scratch with no helper tools at all — like having a kitchen assistant who knows shortcut techniques that get the cooking done faster and use fewer ingredients.

**The thing that does need a real "website/service" is HuggingFace** (where the Qwen3.6 model is kept for download) — for this one you really do have to go online and download the model files the first time (~70GB), but after that they're kept on the rented machine, so you don't have to download them again.

## 5. What is fine-tuning ("tuning")?

The AI model we downloaded (Qwen3.6) has already been trained on general data from millions and millions of pages on the internet — it's good at general things, but it has **never seen our Thai construction drawings at all**, and it doesn't know what form we want the answer in (what kind of JSON, which fields it should have).

**Fine-tuning ("tuning")** means taking this already-capable model and giving it **"extra specialized training"** with our own examples (226 pairs of drawing image + correct answer) so it gets familiar with this particular job — like a doctor who has already finished general medical school and then does extra specialist training to become a bone doctor, without having to redo all of medical school from the very beginning.

---

## 6. What is LoRA?

The model has 35,000 million parameters. If we "train it more" by changing every single number, it would **use an enormous amount of VRAM and risk breaking the model's existing knowledge** (like writing over the entire book, all of it, from scratch).

**LoRA** (short for Low-Rank Adaptation) is a technique that **doesn't change a single one of the original parameters** — instead it creates small "add-on adjusters" and sticks them on alongside — like not writing over the original book, but sticking **small sticky notes (post-it)** on the front of each page we want to change. When the model is about to answer something, it looks at both the original content + the post-its stuck on it, together.

**LORA_R** is the "size of the post-it" — the higher the number (e.g. 64), the bigger the post-it, so you can write things on it in more detail, but it also eats more VRAM. Today it was set to 64 at first, then we ran into the memory-full problem (see section 8), so it was lowered to 32 — the post-it is a bit smaller, but there's still enough room to write what's needed. And research says that when there's little training data (226 examples counts as little), a smaller post-it is actually even more suitable (otherwise there's a risk it "memorizes the wrong way" and ends up losing what it already knew).

**Another advantage:** once training is done, we get a "post-it" file (LoRA adapter) that is **very small** (a few hundred MB), instead of having to save the whole 35,000-million-parameter model all over again (which would weigh tens of GB).

---

## 7. What is MoE (Mixture of Experts) — and why it's related to the problem we hit today

The Qwen3.6 model we use isn't a typical "one-piece brain" — it's an **MoE (it has many sub-experts)** — inside this model there are **256 "sub-experts" (experts)** hidden away. Each time it is about to produce one word of its answer, the model **picks just a few experts** to help answer (it doesn't use all 256 at once every time) — so even though the model is very large (35,000 million parameters), the actual computation only uses ~3,000 million of them each time (faster than if it had to use all of them).

**Why this is related to today's memory-full problem:** when the "LoRA post-its" (section 6) are stuck onto the experts — because there are as many as 256 experts, the post-it has to be stuck on over and over, 256 times (a separate one for each), which eats far more memory than an ordinary model that isn't MoE. This is the real reason memory filled up today.

---

## 8. What is "memory full" (CUDA Out of Memory / OOM)?

The GPU has its own memory (VRAM), separate from the computer's normal RAM — today we rented a GPU with 95GB of VRAM.

When teaching the model (training), several things have to be kept in VRAM at the same time:
1. The model itself (~70GB for 35,000 million parameters)
2. The "LoRA post-its" (sections 6-7)
3. **Intermediate values during computation** (activation memory) — the longer the text/image fed in, the more intermediate values have to be kept

Today the 95GB of VRAM was **almost exactly full** (94.87GB used, only ~100MB left), and when the model needed just a tiny bit more space (100MB), there was none left to find → error "**CUDA out of memory**" (literally: the GPU [the kind that uses a technology called CUDA] has run out of memory).

It's like a backpack packed so tightly that there's only a tiny gap left — when one more item comes along that has to go in (even a tiny one), you just can't cram it in anymore.

**Fixes tried today (in the order we tried them):**
1. Make memory management smarter (cut down the space that's "reserved just in case" but not actually used) — **tried it, not enough**
2. Shrink the "LoRA post-it" (section 6, from 64 down to 32) — **this one actually worked**, because MoE has 256 experts (section 7), so shrinking the post-it multiplies the savings by 256

## 10. How to read the output during training/evaluation — what these numbers mean

**During training, you'll see numbers like this:**
```
{'train_loss': '1.811', 'epoch': '0.177'}
```
- **loss** = "the error value" — the lower the number, the better (the model's guessed answers are getting closer to the real ones). It starts out high, then gradually keeps going down during training
- **epoch** = "a round" — going through every example in the train set once = epoch 1 (today the plan is to train for 3 rounds = `epoch=3`). The number 0.177 means training has gotten through 17.7% of 1 round (because this was only a short 5-step test, not a full round)
- **step** = "a stride" — 1 step = adjusting the model's weights once (it learns from one group of examples, then adjusts)

**When evaluating on the exam set (`eval_fields.py`), you'll see something like this:**
```
JSON valid           : 0/20  (0.0%)
element หาเจอ        : 0/170  (0.0%)
```
- **JSON valid** = of the answers the model gave back, what % are correctly formatted JSON (can be parsed) — before training (baseline) it got 0%, because the model had never seen this format before at all
- **element หาเจอ** (elements found) = out of all the rebar/beam/column items it should find (170 items across the 20 exam examples), how many items the model answered correctly, matching
- **field exact-match** = among the items that were found, what % of each data field (e.g. beam length, rebar size) is answered spot-on

**How to read it:** before training (baseline) these numbers should be very bad (close to 0%) — that's normal, not something broken, because the model has never seen this task before. **The goal is that once training is finished, these numbers must clearly get better.** If you measure after training and it's still as bad as before, that means the training didn't work and we have to go back and fix it

## 12. The confusing incident "why is the log file we asked for missing?" — a lesson about the terminal command queue

After the 5-step test passed (sections 8-9), the order was given to start the **real full 3-epoch training** by typing a new command straight into the same terminal window right away — but when we checked later, it turned out that **the log file for the full training (`full_train.log`) didn't exist at all**, even though we could see the command had been "typed" up on the screen

### How it got this confusing — a simple analogy

The terminal (the black window where you type commands) works as **"a single queue, one thing at a time"**, like a counter clerk who can take orders from only one person at a time — if the first customer (the 5-step test command) hasn't finished paying yet (hasn't finished running), then even if the second customer (the full-training command) has already walked up and said their order, the clerk still won't start on it — they just "remember what was said for now", and only once the earlier queue has truly finished do they start on the new order

What actually happened: even though the 5-step test command had already "finished training" and "finished saving the files", the script itself (`train_qwen36.py`) still had a **final step** that wasn't done yet — having the model try answering 3 real examples (to show the results right away, rather than just looking at the loss number). This step takes a long time (the model really has to "think up the answer" one character at a time), so **the original program hadn't actually finished yet**, even though it looked like it had

### How we checked (investigating like a detective)

We used a command to check the **"process"** (programs currently running on the machine) and found the original program was still running (it had been running for 885 s = almost 15 min, which is longer than a 5-step test should take). Then we checked which file it was writing its log to and found it was still writing to the old file (`test_run.log`), not the new one (`full_train.log`) — clearly confirming that the new command that had been typed **had not actually started running**; it was just being "remembered, waiting in the queue"

### The lesson

When working through a terminal like this, **you have to wait until you actually see the prompt (the sign that it's ready for a new command) come back first** before you can type the next command. If you type ahead of the queue, the command won't vanish (no need to type it again) — it just has to wait for the earlier queue to clear, and then it starts running by itself automatically. A more reliable way to check than just looking at the screen is to check "process" to see whether the earlier job is really still running, like we did in this incident

## 13. The "training finished, then crashed anyway" incident — a lesson about "shared environments"

The full 3-epoch training finished successfully (loss dropped from 2.0 to 0.14 — very good) **and the output file (LoRA adapter) was saved successfully too**, but right after that, the bonus step at the end of the script (having the model try answering 3 examples to show the results) **went and crashed with a file-not-found error**

### The cause — Claude's own mistake

While waiting for the training, Claude opened another, separate terminal window to prepare the `llama.cpp` program in advance (for the step of converting the model to GGUF that we had talked about) and then ran `pip install` (Python's software installer) in that window

**The problem was:** both windows (training the model + preparing llama.cpp) **used the same "storeroom"** (the same Python environment for the whole machine) — when llama.cpp's `pip install` went and installed/updated the library called `transformers` (the main library used to run AI models), it ended up overwriting the original version that Unsloth had prepared specifically for this model (the one that contains the file supporting Qwen3.5 MoE), turning it into the generic standard version that **doesn't have that file** — so when the training script (which was still running, not finished yet) had to go back and use that file again after saving (when it was about to show the 3 examples), it couldn't find it and crashed

### A simple comparison

It's like 2 people using the same "toolbox" at the same time — one (the training job) is in the middle of using a particular screwdriver, and the other (the llama.cpp prep job) goes and swaps that very same screwdriver for a different model while the first one is still using it. When the first person reaches for the screwdriver to carry on (the same familiar one), it has already turned into a different one

### The key lesson

**Never install/modify shared software (pip install) while another important job is running on the same machine.** If you want to do side work while waiting, you must keep a clearly separate "storeroom" (a technique called a **virtual environment**), not the same room as the main job that is currently running — luckily, this time the important output file (LoRA adapter) had finished saving just before the crash; otherwise we might have had to redo all the training (wasting time + money for nothing)

**Impact:** we now have to repair the environment back to a correct state first, before we can measure the model's quality (`eval_fields.py --adapter`) as planned

## 4. 0.3 — A "dress rehearsal" on your own computer first (local dry run)

This is the step that prevents the 21st's problem most directly. Explaining each term afresh:

### What is GGUF
An AI model in its normal form (during training) is stored in a format that takes up a lot of space and only runs on powerful graphics cards. **GGUF** is a "file format" that compresses a model to be smaller and runnable on an ordinary computer (even without a very powerful graphics card) — the final goal of this whole project is to get a GGUF file to use on Makham's own computer

### What is Quantization (compression)
A model stores numbers at very fine precision (e.g. bf16 = 16-bit numbers). **Quantization** is rounding the numbers to be coarser (e.g. 4-bit) so the file becomes much smaller, in exchange for a small loss of accuracy — like compressing a high-quality JPEG photo into a low-quality JPEG: much smaller file, the picture is still recognizable, but a little detail is lost

### What is mmproj — and why it was the big problem on the 21st
The model we use must **"be able to read images"**, not just read text — the part that "translates images into numbers the model can understand" is a separate file called **mmproj**. On the 21st this file couldn't be split out (the model was too new, the tooling didn't support it), leaving us with a GGUF that could only read text and couldn't read construction drawings at all — **even though that is this project's main job**

**The good news discovered afterwards:** because the training script (`train_qwen36.py`) is configured to **"never touch the image-reading part"** (`finetune_vision_layers=False` — it teaches language only and never touches the image-reading part), the image-reading part of the tuned model is **100% identical to the original model** — so there's no need to try "splitting out a new file" any more; just download the mmproj file the model's maker already made (899MB) and use it together with our own tuned GGUF — the problem that was stuck on the 21st is solved without having to depend on tooling that doesn't support it any more

### What is llama.cpp
**llama.cpp** is the program used to "run" a GGUF file on an ordinary computer — you have to feed it both the model file (GGUF) and the mmproj file together if you want it to read images

### What was actually done today (rehearsing the whole process before spending money on a rental)

## 5. 0.4 — Re-reading the script seriously, and finding a bug nobody knew about before

### What is a token (new term today)
An AI model doesn't read one letter at a time or one word at a time the way a person reads — it cuts text (and images) into small pieces called **tokens**, then processes them one piece at a time. The longer/more detailed the text/image, the more tokens there are

**A single image counts as tokens too** — a more detailed image gets cut into more tokens (this model's formula: the largest image size we set = about 5,120 tokens/image)

### What is MAX_LENGTH — why it's a bug
The training script has to set "the maximum length allowed per example" (`MAX_LENGTH`) in advance — if any example is longer than the limit, the program will **"cut the end off"**, dropping whatever runs over

The problem found today: the original value (9,216 tokens) **was worked out from examples with only 1 image** — but the real dataset has 5 special examples (called "grid master" — 1 per house, teaching the grid lines of the whole house) that use **2-4 images** at once — counted for real, these examples need as much as ~22,000 tokens (2.4 times more than what was set)

**What this means:** if not fixed, during real training the program would cut the end off these examples — and the end of an example is **the correct answer we want to teach the model** (not just trimming off some unneeded part), which amounts to teaching the model with answers that have pieces missing. And as it happens, these examples are the only data that teaches "the grid lines of the whole house" (the very spot the script itself says is the most frequently missed part) — if left as is, this tuning round would teach this topic wrong from the start

### The options available + what Makham chose
There were 4 fixes (raise the allowed size / lower the image resolution for this group only / leave it as it was / drop this group of examples entirely) — **Makham chose to raise the allowed size** (from 9,216 to 24,576) so that no image detail is lost at all

**The trade-off you need to know:** this higher value means that when training these 5 examples specifically, there is **a higher chance of running out of memory (OOM, see section 8 in Part 1)**, because last round the memory had only ~100MB left out of 95GB when this was set at 9,216 — if OOM hits at this point, the fix is to shrink the "LoRA post-it" further, or lower the image resolution for just these 5 examples — not to lower this value back to the original (because that would go back to cutting the answers off again)

**Key point:** this bug **existed even before house 05 was added** — nobody had ever caught it, because nobody had ever read the script against the real numbers in the dataset this carefully. This is why Rule 4 (section 1) says you must **"actually do it and check it for real, not just skim through it"**

## 3. Reading the loss curve during training — what is loss, and what does it tell us

**loss** is a number telling you "how wrong the model's guesses are" in each round — **the lower the better** (0 = guessed exactly right, a high number = guessing wildly). Today we saw the real values throughout training:

```
start: 1.795 → falling steadily → ending around 0.18-0.21
```

A downward path like this is a good sign — it means the model **is genuinely learning**, not standing still or getting worse (if loss doesn't fall at all, or bounces up and down with no direction = a sign something is wrong, e.g. the learning rate is too high)

**But low loss ≠ genuinely usable** — this is the key point the whole `rule_of_tune.md` file warns about (lesson 4 of the old CLAUDE.md file). Loss only measures "how close the guess is to the answer mathematically"; it does not measure **whether the rebar numbers were really read correctly, or whether the part names were read correctly** — you need a Phase 7-style measurement (counting the fields that genuinely match, one by one) to know the real answer

## 4. VRAM going up and down during training — why

## 7. Why Phase 7.5 has to download a whole model from scratch — what exactly is a LoRA adapter?

The question Makham asked today: "why is loading taking so long?" — the answer connects to an understanding of **LoRA** that's worth knowing a little more deeply

Remember from the earlier files that LoRA is like **"a Post-it note stuck on top"**? — here's the real reason: LoRA **doesn't store a whole new model**; it only stores a **small "difference"** that says "when you run into a situation like this, adjust the answer a little in this direction" — t01's adapter file is only ~7.5GB, while the full model (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B) weighs as much as ~70GB

## 9. The biggest story of the day — the whole day's results flipped because of "a value that wasn't set", not "a value that was set wrong"

### What was found

After Phase 7.5 finished, I ordered another very thorough round of checking (43 agents working together to find holes) — and found something big: **t01 (Qwen3.6) had been trained on images ~14 times blurrier than t02 (Qwen3-VL)**, without anyone intending it

### How could this happen when it had already been checked so thoroughly?

Remember **"bug 2"** found during Phase 0.4 (when reading the scripts before renting the machine)? — back then we found that if `resize="max"` isn't set explicitly, the program quietly shrinks images down to 512px behind your back, **and that problem was fixed — but only on t02's script side**. Nobody thought to go back and check whether t01's script (written back last month) had the same problem

**Why the carefully made comparison table (parity table) didn't catch it:** because that table only compared **"values that were deliberately set"** (e.g. learning rate, batch size, number of epochs) — it didn't compare **"values left at their initial value (default) without typing anything at all"**, and `resize="max"` is the latter kind: t02's side typed it out explicitly, t01's side typed nothing at all (because it was written before anyone knew this problem existed). With no line to compare, nobody noticed they were different

**Analogy:** it's like checking two recipes to see whether they use the same amount of salt/sugar/chili (the things written down in the recipe) but forgetting to check "how strong the heat is", even though nobody wrote the heat level into either recipe (each used its stove's own default heat, which differed) — so when the dishes came out tasting different, you'd guess it was because the ingredients differed, when really the heat was different from the very start

### Confirmed by real measurement, not just by reading the code and guessing

Actually loaded the Qwen3.6 model, built the "image shrinker" (collator) exactly the same way `train_qwen36.py` does (no resize=), then fed in 1 real image — the program itself printed:

```
Unsloth: Model does not have a default image size - using 512
```

and counted that only **~266 visual tokens** were left, versus the **~3,796-5,100 tokens** t02 trained with (~14 times different) — this is **proof**, not a guess

### Why this overturns everything concluded earlier

All day the conclusion had been "Qwen3.6 reads Thai construction drawings better than Qwen3-VL" — now it has to change to **"Qwen3.6, seeing far blurrier images, still did better than Qwen3-VL seeing sharp ones"**, which is a different conclusion — the first answers the question set at the very start (which family is better); the second can't, because the experiment was unfair from the start (like making one runner wear ankle weights and then racing them: if the one wearing weights still wins, they really are good, but you can't tell "how much faster they'd be without the weights")

### The options offered + what Makham chose

Offered 2 ways: **(A)** stop here and accept the results with a clear warning attached, or **(B)** retrain t01 with `resize="max"` so it sees images as sharp as t02 did, then re-measure for a genuinely fair comparison (costs more time + money). **Makham chose (A)** — accept the results as they are, with the warning attached everywhere in the documents, no retraining

### The lesson recorded permanently

Added as **Lesson 12** in `rule_of_tune.md`: **the variable-comparison table for an A/B experiment must list every argument passed into the shared functions/classes, not just the values deliberately chosen** — a value left at its default is a variable too, and because there's no line to compare, it's the easiest one to miss. Next round, every argument must actually be compared from the source code, not just the list of things you remember changing

llama.cpp even warned us itself in the log (but nobody read it):
```
W load_hparams: Qwen-VL models require at minimum 1024 image tokens to function correctly
W load_hparams: if you encounter problems with accuracy, try adding --image-min-tokens 1024
```

**Sub-lesson:** warnings printed while a model loads usually get overlooked because they show up mixed in with hundreds of other lines — **this is the same pattern repeating for the 2nd time** (the first time was the collator printing `Model does not have a default image size - using 512` and nobody seeing it — exactly the same)

### 5.2 Compression (quantization) that the model's own makers warned against

**What quantization is:** a model is billions of numbers, normally stored at high precision (bf16). **Quantization** = lowering the precision so the file is small enough to run on a small machine. `Q4_K_M` → from 70GB **down to 21GB**

> Like compressing a JPEG — compress a little and you barely see a difference; compress a lot and blocky blur starts to show

**The `t01_workflow.md` document itself says** that Unsloth (the people who make the model) warns this family *"loses quality abnormally once quantized"* — which is why we **forced training in bf16 and banned 4-bit**

**But when it came time to use it, we compressed it to Q4_K_M anyway** (out of necessity — a laptop with 8GB of VRAM can't handle 70GB) **and never went back to re-measure whether it was still accurate after compression**

→ **we were careful about this during training, but forgot to be careful during use**

## 7. Why it's slow — the real numbers and the reasons

| What | Speed |
|---|---|
| Reading the image + prompt | 3,924 tokens @ ~28 t/s = **~140 seconds** |
| Writing the answer | **3.46 tokens/second** |
| Full extraction of 1 page | ~13-21 minutes |
| Classifying 1 page | ~2.4 minutes |

### The reason — simple arithmetic
```
Model file  21 GB
VRAM        8 GB      → only ~1 in 3 fits on the graphics card; the rest runs on the CPU
```
The log confirms it directly: `failed to fit params to free device memory ... abort`

**An interesting detail:** the GPU shows 100% but draws only **33.7W** (this card can draw far more than that) — meaning the card **isn't working hard; it's just waiting on the CPU the whole time**. The CPU is the real bottleneck

### Why classifying is 7 times faster than full extraction, even though it looks at the same image

Because most of the time goes into **"writing the answer"**, not "looking at the image"
```
classify:          look at image 140 s + write ["plan","schedule"] ~10 tokens = 3 s    → ~144 s
full extraction:   look at image 140 s + write a long JSON ~3,800 tokens = 1,100 s     → ~1,250 s
```
**At 3.46 tokens/second, every token that has to be written is real time lost** → this is why the 2-phase plan (scan quickly first, then extract only the pages needed) genuinely helps — see Appendix A for how `extract_house01_local.py` actually does this 2-phase run

## 10. Summary of tonight's lessons — 6 to remember

1. **"Tuned successfully" ≠ "usable"**. Between the two lie several more layers: merge → quantize → a different program to run it. **Every layer can change the results, and every layer has to be measured again**

2. **A check gate that's too easy to pass is worse than no gate at all**, because it gives false confidence. `export_gguf.py:107` showed a green ✓ from the 24th and we believed it all along. **A check gate has to ask "if the thing really were broken, would this gate catch it?"** — if the answer is "no", that gate is worthless

3. **Notice "the pattern of the mistakes", not just "how many mistakes"**. Tonight the most important clue wasn't "lots of mistakes" but **"wrong on the easy things, right on the hard things"**, which is unnatural → that led to finding the real cause. If you only look at the total score, you'd never find it

4. **An accuracy figure must come with the question "measured on what?"** Our 90% was measured with bf16 + an unmerged adapter — not the thing that actually runs on the machine

5. **The input during use must match the input during training** (the formal term: *train/inference mismatch*). Once they differ 14 times over, what was learned in training stops working immediately

6. **Warnings the program prints must be read** — we've now hit this pattern twice. Next time, read through the warnings at model load every time before starting a long run

# Appendix B — the real prompts sent to Qwen (t01)

**Know this before reading:** the Qwen in this appendix is **not** Alibaba DashScope's Qwen-VL-Max that the Constistant app (`js/ai/qwenVision.js` + the Supabase Edge Function `qwen-vision`) calls through the cloud — that one is a different, online-service model, with a different pipeline and a different prompt (that side's prompts are in `js/ai/prompts.js` in the `Constistant` repo). The Qwen in this document is **`Qwen3.6-35B-A3B`, which Makham tuned personally (t01) and runs as a GGUF file on Makham's own machine** through `llama-server` — a different model, running in a different place, with a different prompt, but with the same goal (read Thai construction drawings and extract the data as JSON)

There are 2 real prompts that `extract_house01_local.py` sends to the t01 model (they come from the 2-phase pipeline explained in Part 4, item 7 — full extraction of every page was too slow, ~18 minutes/page, so it was split into a quick scan first, then extracting only the pages needed):

## B.1 Phase A — `CLASSIFY_PROMPT` (a quick scan that asks only for the pattern of each view)

Sent with the image of 1 page, for the model to answer briefly (limited by `MAX_TOKENS_CLASSIFY = 300`), so we know which kinds of views that page has before deciding whether to send it into Phase B (full extraction) or skip it:

```
You are looking at one page of a Thai reinforced-concrete construction drawing set.
Identify every distinct view/box on this page. Reply with ONLY a JSON array of pattern
strings, one entry per view (same order as they appear on the page), using exactly one
of these values per entry: plan, section, schedule, notes, index, material_list,
site_plan, side_profile, gridline, title, symbol, roof_plan, misc, unknown.
Example: ["plan", "schedule"]
No commentary, no markdown fence, JSON array only.
```

Only pages whose classification includes a pattern in `KEEP_PATTERNS = {"plan", "section", "roof_plan", "side_profile", "gridline"}` are sent into Phase B — the rest (schedule, notes, index, material_list, site_plan, title, symbol, misc, unknown) are skipped, which saves a lot of time

## B.2 Phase B — `PROMPT_SHORT` (full extraction — the same prompt used to teach the model during training)

This is the main prompt of the whole project — **the most important thing about it is that it's "byte-identical" in all 3 places**: the instruction that taught the model during training (`PROMPT_SHORT` in `build_dataset.js`), the instruction used for evaluation (`eval_fields.py`), and the instruction used for real extraction (`extract_house01_local.py` — a comment in that file itself notes "byte-identical to PROMPT_SHORT in tune_ai/t01/data_before_tune/build_dataset.js"). This exact sameness must always be preserved — teaching one thing and asking another (a train/inference prompt mismatch) would make the results measured so far meaningless, just like the "image resolution during training vs during use differs 14 times" problem found in Part 4 (a different spot: that one is the "images" not matching; this one is the "instructions" that have to match) — sent with the image of 1 page, with the longest answer limited to `MAX_TOKENS_EXTRACT = 9000`:

```
You are reading one page of a Thai reinforced-concrete (RC) construction drawing set.
Extract everything on the page into JSON following the primary_rawjson_schema.

Inventory EVERY view/box on the page first, then emit one entry per view in "views"
(a single-view page still uses a one-entry array — never drop a view). Each view
carries its own "pattern": plan, section, schedule, notes, index, material_list,
site_plan, side_profile, gridline, title, symbol, roof_plan, misc, or unknown.

GRID AND DUMMY GRID — the single most error-prone part of this task, read carefully:
- grid_ref reads row-letter first, then column ("A-1", not "1-A"). Point-type elements
  (footing/column) use a grid_refs array instead of start/end.
- A structural line not on a named/printed grid still needs a name: append a prime to
  the nearest named grid ("1'", "A'"). If more than one dummy line falls in the same
  gap, number them in reading order (left→right / top→bottom): 1st gets one prime,
  2nd gets two.
- THE KEY RULE: if a beam's start or end point does not sit on any grid line you can
  see, that point still needs a grid line — it does NOT mean the beam should be
  dropped. Trace every beam segment, including short stubs near stairs/closets. For
  each endpoint: use the existing named/dummy grid if one is there; if not, read its
  position off a printed dimension chain and record a new dummy grid, then reference
  the beam against it. Never: (a) drop the beam because it "isn't on the grid",
  (b) write a prose description instead of grid_ref_start/grid_ref_end, (c) set
  start=end with a null span. Exception: a slab/eave edge with no beam label and no
  corner columns is not structural and needs no dummy grid.
- Span length comes from the grid table, not your own visual estimate.

REBAR (main_bar):
- Always split top/bottom, even when the counts are equal — never collapse into one.
- If a section shows a clearly distinct row of bars at mid-depth (own leader line,
  sitting between the top and bottom rows, usually a deep beam), record it as a third
  face, main_bar.middle. Do not fold it into additional_bars, and do not invent one by
  splitting a top/bottom cluster.
- A circle symbol (Ø) always means round bar (RB); visible ribs mean deformed bar (DB)
  — read the symbol, never infer type from diameter.
- Columns use a single main_bar.count for the 4 corner bars — do not split top/bottom.
- Before assigning an "additional" bar to top or bottom, check the leader line itself,
  not just the label wording — the same-looking label has resolved to opposite sides
  on different marks in this series.

OUTPUT DISCIPLINE:
- Same element_id appearing more than once on this page with non-overlapping
  positions → merge into one entry (sum count, concatenate grid_refs). Exception: a
  multi-level schedule keeps the same element_id per level as separate entries, using
  a "level" field — never embed the level into element_id.
- One atomic entry per grid-to-grid beam segment; do not pre-group same-mark spans.
- Reading order: top-to-bottom by row, left-to-right by column, vertical before
  horizontal at a shared start point.
- Use null for anything unclear. Do not guess or invent a value.

Reply with JSON only. No markdown fence, no commentary.
```

**Why the instruction looks like this and not some other way:** it isn't written as the full spec (~6,000 tokens), nor as a short summary that gives every rule equal weight — it was measured against the real `workmen's_diary/` (grep-count 2026-07-20): how many times each topic was corrected/mentioned over 3 weeks, and space in the prompt was given according to that frequency: dummy grid + beam endpoints (mentioned 48 times) got the most space, main_bar top/bottom (50 times combined) got a lot of space, while confidence/price-table/specs join (≤16 times) were cut out entirely, because the dataset has nothing to teach them with anyway — full details are in `tune_ai/t01/data_before_tune/README.md` under the heading "instruction ~1,020 tok"

**⚠️ Directly related to Part 4:** this very prompt is the reason the `export_gguf.py:107` check gate (which checks whether the words `views`/`pattern` appear in the answer) can't tell "a tuned model" from "a bare model that is just following the prompt" — because the words `views` and `pattern` **are stated right in the prompt itself**, so any model can produce them even if it was never tuned at all (see Part 4, item 4, Evidence B). This is why the next round's check gate must look for words that are **not in the prompt** (e.g. `png`/`doc_page`, which exist only in the GT) instead
